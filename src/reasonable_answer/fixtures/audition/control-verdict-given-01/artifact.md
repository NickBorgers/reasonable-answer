## Conclusion

Of the open letter's four allegations, one is backed by the record, one is half backed, one is neither confirmed nor ruled out by any record this report could find, and one is a judgement of motive that no record could settle [1]. The repair backlog is real and the letter's figure for it is conservative: the City Auditor counted 1,340 open work orders in mid-2023, more than a fifth of them over a year old [2]. The authority did spend capital money on its headquarters, but its own expenditure report records that spending as a separately budgeted line, not as money moved out of apartment repairs [3]. The retaliation allegation has no record behind it in either direction [4][5]. The strongest objection to these verdicts is that a count of logged work orders undercounts what tenants have stopped reporting; that is engaged below, and it strengthens the first verdict rather than weakening it.

## Key findings

- The letter makes four allegations: a backlog of more than 1,000 open repair requests, a diversion of apartment-repair money to the authority's headquarters, lease non-renewals in retaliation for complaints, and a leadership that "does not care" about tenant safety [1].
- The City Auditor counted 1,340 open work orders on 30 June 2023 after removing duplicate orders for the same unit and defect, and found 290 of them, about 22 percent, open for more than twelve months [2].
- The authority's capital fund spent $1.2 million of $4.1 million on renovating its headquarters in fiscal year 2023, under a line of the adopted capital plan separate from unit repairs [3].
- The unit-repair line was underspent by $0.9 million in the same year, which the auditor attributes to unfilled maintenance positions rather than to a transfer [2].
- The Fair Housing Commission's complaint report records no retaliation complaint against the authority in 2022 or 2023 [4].
- The authority's written response denies that any lease was declined because of a complaint and gives no count of non-renewals [5].

## The strongest counterargument

The strongest case against these verdicts is that the records they rest on count what was filed, not what happened. The auditor's figure is a count of logged work orders [2], so a tenant who stopped reporting a leak after the third unanswered request is not in it; the commission's report lists only complaints someone chose to bring [4]. On that reading every verdict above leans toward the authority, because every record used is one the tenants had to start.

That objection is right about the records and changes less than it seems. For the backlog it cuts in the letter's favour: an undercount makes "more than 1,000" more true, not less, which is why that allegation is called backed rather than merely plausible. For retaliation it is the reason the verdict is "not found" rather than "false": an empty complaint record cannot rule out conduct nobody reported [4]. What would settle both is a survey of tenants' unreported defects and a list of non-renewals with their stated grounds. Neither appears in any source cited here.

## The backlog and the headquarters spending

The letter's first allegation is that more than 1,000 repair requests are open and that some have waited over a year [1]. The auditor's count of 1,340 open work orders at the end of June 2023, 290 of them older than twelve months, backs both halves; the auditor removed duplicate orders before counting, so the figure is not inflated by repeat requests for one defect [2]. The authority's response disputes the letter's description of the backlog but gives no count of its own [5]. The audit predates the letter by eight months, so it backs the claim as of mid-2023 and says nothing about the months since.

The second allegation is that money budgeted for apartment repairs was spent on renovating the authority's headquarters [1]. Half of it holds. The expenditure report shows $1.2 million spent on the headquarters in fiscal year 2023 [3]. But the same report lists that renovation as its own line in the adopted capital plan, approved before the year began, and the unit-repair line shows no transfer out of it [3]. The auditor found that line underspent by $0.9 million and traced the shortfall to vacant maintenance positions [2]. The spending happened; on these records, the diversion did not.

## Retaliation, and the claim that needs no verdict

The third allegation is that tenants who complained publicly had their leases declined at renewal [1]. This report looked for a record of it in two places: the Fair Housing Commission's annual complaint report, which lists every complaint by respondent and basis [4], and the authority's written response, which answers the letter point by point [5]. The commission records no retaliation complaint against the authority in 2022 or 2023 [4]. The response denies retaliation and gives no figures [5]. Nothing found confirms the allegation and nothing found rules it out; on the public record it is unresolved.

The fourth allegation, that the authority's leadership "does not care" whether tenants live in safe conditions, is a judgement about motive and attitude [1]. No record could confirm or refute it, and this report gives it no verdict. The factual premises it leans on, the backlog and the use of capital money, are assessed above on their own terms.
