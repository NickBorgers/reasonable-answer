## Conclusion

The 2019 law expanded who is covered much further than it expanded who takes leave: 92 percent of the state's wage workforce clears the qualifying-earnings threshold [1], while workers in the bottom wage quintile filed claims at 34 percent of the top quintile's rate across 2020-2023 [2]. Most of that distance is attributable to how the benefit is delivered rather than to whom the statute names — the wage-replacement schedule, and a job-protection guarantee the 2019 law does not itself contain [3][5]. The strongest contrary reading, that the filing gap reflects different leave needs across the wage distribution rather than a barrier, is engaged below: it accounts for part of the gap and not for its concentration among small-employer workers [1][2].

## Key findings

- The statute covers every employee who earned at least $2,600 in the qualifying period, which the auditor puts at 92 percent of the state's wage workforce [1][3].
- Claims per thousand covered workers in the bottom wage quintile ran at 34 percent of the top quintile's rate across 2020-2023 [2].
- Wage replacement runs from 90 percent of the first tranche of weekly wages down to 60 percent above it, so a worker whose hours vary sees a replacement rate that varies with them [3].
- The 2019 law creates a benefit but no right to reinstatement; job protection comes from the older state family-leave statute, which reaches only employers of 50 or more [3].
- Employers must notify employees of the benefit and no agency audits that they do; a business-association survey found 61 percent of responding firms under 50 employees had no written notice in place [5].
- Self-employed workers are covered only if they elect in, and an electing worker pays both the employee and the employer premium share [3][4].

## The strongest counterargument

The strongest case that access did expand, and that the filing gap measures something other than a barrier, is that low-wage jobs carry shorter tenure and more part-year work, so fewer of those workers reach a qualifying event while covered at all. The state's own data supports part of this: the auditor's tenure-adjusted comparison narrows the gap from 34 percent of the top-quintile rate to 51 percent, which is a large share of it [1]. What the explanation does not reach is where the remaining gap sits. Within the bottom quintile, workers at firms of 50 or more filed at nearly twice the rate of workers doing comparable work at smaller firms, and the department's data shows firm size is not standing in for tenure — median tenure differs by under two months across that boundary [2]. A needs-based account therefore covers part of the distance and the 50-employee line covers more of what is left; neither on its own explains the pattern, and this report treats both as operating.

## Coverage on paper

Coverage is the one dimension on which the 2019 law did what it was written to do. The qualifying-earnings threshold of $2,600 is low enough that the auditor counts 92 percent of the state's wage workforce inside it, including 88 percent of workers in the bottom wage quintile [1]. No prior state leave program reached that far down the wage distribution [1].

Coverage and realized access are separate measures, and the distance between them is where this question is actually decided. Being named by the statute puts a benefit within a worker's reach on paper; whether the leave can be afforded, and whether there is a job at the end of it, is settled by the replacement schedule and by a different statute entirely [3][5]. The two groups with the least realized use arrive there by different routes, described below, so the filing gap is not one phenomenon with one explanation [1][4].

## What converts coverage into a leave taken

Coverage entitles a worker to file. What determines whether filing is affordable is the replacement schedule, which pays 90 percent of the first tranche of weekly wages and 60 percent above it [3]. Applied to a paycheck with little slack in it, the shortfall is the part of the rent that does not get paid, and the auditor's claimant survey records it as the most-cited reason for shortening a leave already begun [1].

Job protection is the sharper boundary, and it is not in the 2019 law at all. Reinstatement rights come from the older family-leave statute, which applies to employers of 50 or more, so a covered worker at a smaller firm draws a benefit with no statutory right to the job at the end of it [3]. Notification runs along the same line: 61 percent of responding firms under 50 employees had no written notice of the benefit in place, against 12 percent of larger ones [5].

## The two groups with the least realized use

The two groups are outside the program in different senses. Hourly employees at firms below the 50-employee threshold are inside it — covered, premium-paying, entitled to the benefit — and hold it without a statutory right to return; the advocacy review's case files describe workers in that position filing and then withdrawing claims once a supervisor's reaction made the risk concrete [4]. Self-employed workers are outside it by default and must elect in, paying both premium shares to do so; among the elections that review followed, most were made by contractors with steady annual billings rather than by intermittent ones [4]. The auditor reports the same split from the administrative side, as a low claim rate at small employers and a low election rate among the self-employed, and treats them as separate series [1].
