## Conclusion

Protected bike lanes are associated with lower traffic fatalities for all road users, not only cyclists, and the association is large enough and consistent enough to act on [1]. The mechanism is contested: separated infrastructure plausibly works by calming general traffic and lowering speeds, but the same cities that build it also do many other things, and no cited study separates those [4][5]. The strongest opposing view is that the causal arrow runs partly the other way — cities become safe and then build lanes, or simply have more cyclists, which is independently protective [3] — and nothing below rules that out. Read as "cities that build protected lanes tend to be safer," the evidence is strong; read as "building a lane will lower fatalities by a predictable amount," it is not yet there.

## Key findings

- Across 13 large US cities over 13 years, cities that added protected bike lanes saw larger declines in total road fatalities than cities that did not, including among drivers and pedestrians [1].
- Cities in the top quartile of protected-lane construction had fatality rates roughly 44 percent below the sample mean by the end of that period [1].
- A case-crossover study of cyclist injuries found separated cycle tracks carried a small fraction of the injury risk of a reference major street with parked cars and no bicycle infrastructure [2].
- Injury risk per cyclist falls as the number of cyclists rises, so any infrastructure that increases cycling produces a safety improvement that is not attributable to the infrastructure's physical design [3].
- Lane narrowing and vertical elements at the roadway edge have documented independent effects on vehicle speed, and speed is the dominant input to fatality risk [4][5].

## The strongest counterargument

The serious objection is reverse causation and selection working together. A city that installs protected lanes is a city whose politics already favour lower speed limits, daylighted intersections, enforcement, and transit — and every one of those independently reduces fatalities [5]. The cross-city comparison in [1] cannot separate the lane from the municipal programme it arrives with, so the coefficient on lane-miles may be measuring the programme.

Jacobsen's safety-in-numbers result sharpens the objection rather than answering it: risk per cyclist falls as cycling rises, so a lane that attracts riders improves the per-cyclist rate whether or not its physical design does anything [3]. What would have to hold for the conclusion above to weaken materially is that the lane-specific effect shrinks toward zero once the accompanying programme is controlled for. The case-crossover evidence in [2] is the main reason to doubt that it goes all the way to zero: it compares route types travelled by the same rider, which does not depend on which city built what.

## The association

Marshall and Ferenchak examined 13 large US cities over 13 years and found that cities which added protected bike lanes saw larger declines in total road fatalities than cities which did not, including fatalities among drivers and pedestrians [1]. The effect size is substantial: cities in the top quartile of protected-lane construction saw fatality rates roughly 44 percent below the sample mean by the end of the period [1].

Teschke and colleagues approach the same question from the individual rather than the city, using a case-crossover design in which each injured cyclist's route is compared with a control route the same person rode. Separated cycle tracks carried a small fraction of the risk of a reference major street with parked cars and no bicycle infrastructure [2].

## Interpreting the association

Protected lanes physically narrow the general traffic lane and introduce vertical elements at the roadway edge. Both are established traffic-calming measures with documented independent effects on vehicle speed, and speed is the dominant input to fatality risk in the crash literature [4][5].

Because cities with more protected bike lanes have lower fatality rates, building protected bike lanes will reduce fatalities in any city that builds them.

## What would strengthen the case

Staggered-adoption designs exploiting the timing of individual lane installations would separate the lane effect from the general municipal-safety-investment effect [5]. Several such studies are underway; none cited here has reported.

Failing that, dose-response evidence within a single city — fatality changes on corridors that received lanes against comparable corridors in the same city and the same political programme — would hold the confound fixed without waiting for a natural experiment [4].
