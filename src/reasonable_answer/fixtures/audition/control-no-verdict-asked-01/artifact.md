## Conclusion

The city housing authority sorts every repair request into one of three priority tiers by what the defect threatens, and works each tier to its own deadline: emergencies within a day, urgent defects within a week, routine ones within thirty days [1]. Requests stay open for more than a year when they are routine and either wait behind a rising volume of emergencies for maintenance staff the authority does not have, or cost enough to need a contract that takes months to let [2]. The two causes overlap rather than compete: the auditor found most year-old orders in the routine tier and a disproportionate share of them above the contracting threshold [2]. The strongest objection, that the long waits are the priority system working as designed rather than a shortage, is engaged below; it is partly right, and the auditor's figures show where it stops [2][3].

## Key findings

- The authority's maintenance manual sets three tiers: emergency (a threat to life or to the building, answered within 24 hours), urgent (loss of an essential service, within 7 days) and routine (everything else, a 30-day target) [1].
- Any repair estimated above $25,000 must be competitively bid under the authority's procurement policy, and the board approves the resulting contract [1][5].
- On 30 June 2023 the City Auditor counted 1,340 open work orders, 290 of them open for more than twelve months; 251 of those 290 were routine-tier [2].
- Emergency orders rose from 21 percent to 34 percent of the maintenance workload between fiscal years 2021 and 2023 [2].
- An average of 18 of the authority's 64 maintenance positions were vacant across fiscal year 2023 [2][4].
- The median time from a repair estimate above the threshold to a signed contract was 94 days [2].

## The strongest counterargument

The strongest objection to this account is that it treats as a shortage what is really a rule. A tier system that answers emergencies first will, whenever emergencies rise, push routine work back without limit; a routine request that waits a year is the manual doing what it says [1]. On that reading more staff would shorten the wait only until emergency volume rose again, and the cause of year-long waits is the priority rule itself.

That is partly right, and the manual contains no ceiling on how long a routine order may be deferred [1]. But the auditor's figures show the rule alone does not produce the waits. The authority's capital report shows the unit-repair budget underspent by $0.9 million in the same year that routine orders aged, which is money the rule did not stop anyone from spending [3]. The auditor attributes that underspend to vacancies [2]. A rule that orders the queue and a shortage that slows it are both operating; neither on its own accounts for the 290 year-old orders.

## How a request moves through the tiers

A request is logged by the development's management office, which assigns the tier from the manual's list of defects: gas leaks, fire-safety failures and structural damage are emergencies; loss of heat, hot water or a working toilet is urgent; everything else, from a leaking tap to damaged plaster, is routine [1]. Staff are dispatched by tier and then by age within it, so a routine order is reached only once no emergency or urgent order is waiting for the same trade [1].

Work estimated above $25,000 leaves this queue altogether. It is bid under the procurement policy, and the resulting contract goes to the board for approval at a regular meeting [1]; the minutes of the March 2024 meeting record a slate of repair contracts approved together [5]. A request whose estimate crosses the threshold therefore also waits for a board meeting.

## Why some requests wait more than a year

Three things combine in the year-old orders. Most are routine: 251 of the 290 orders open for over a year sat in the lowest tier [2]. Emergency volume rose over the same period, from about a fifth to about a third of the workload, which is the volume routine orders were waiting behind [2]. And the staff to absorb it were not there: the human resources department's vacancy report and the auditor's review both put the average at 18 of 64 maintenance positions unfilled through fiscal year 2023 [2][4].

The contracting route adds a separate delay. The auditor found a median of 94 days between an estimate above $25,000 and a signed contract, and orders above the threshold made up a larger share of the year-old orders than of all open orders [2]. A request that is both routine and expensive therefore waits twice, once for the staff to estimate it and once for the contract.
